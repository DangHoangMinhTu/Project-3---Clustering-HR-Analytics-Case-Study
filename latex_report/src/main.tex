\begin{abstract}
This report applies unsupervised learning to the \emph{HR Analytics Case Study}
dataset, which describes about 4{,}410 employees of a company through
demographic, job, survey and attendance (swipe-in/swipe-out) records.

The goal is to discover naturally occurring employee segments without using
any pre-defined label. We integrate the relational files, clean and engineer
features (including average daily working hours derived from attendance logs),
and compare three clustering algorithms --- K-Means, Hierarchical (Ward)
clustering and DBSCAN --- using the Silhouette score, the Elbow method,
Davies--Bouldin and Calinski--Harabasz indices.

The resulting segments are profiled and cross-checked against the
\texttt{Attrition} column (used only for external validation) to derive
practical HR recommendations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Problem Definition}

Employee turnover is costly: every employee who leaves has to be replaced,
recruited and trained again, and experience is lost with them. Human
Resources (HR) departments therefore want to know \emph{which kinds of
employees} exist in the organisation and which of them need attention. The
company in this case study has no such grouping; it only has raw records
about each employee.

This project uses \textbf{unsupervised learning (clustering)} to discover
naturally occurring groups of employees with similar career, workload,
satisfaction and commute characteristics. No label is given to the
algorithms: the goal is not to predict a known outcome, but to find structure
that HR can act on. The recorded \texttt{Attrition} (whether the employee left
the company) is deliberately \emph{not} used for clustering; it is kept aside
to check afterwards whether the discovered groups are meaningful.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which employee characteristics carry enough structure to separate
  groups of employees?
  \item Which clustering algorithm (K-Means, Hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN)
  and how many clusters give the best and most reliable segmentation?
  \item How can the segments be described, and do they differ in attrition?
  \item What practical actions can HR derive from the segments?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset}

The \emph{HR Analytics Case Study} dataset from Kaggle describes 4,410
employees of one company in five files: general information (demographics,
job, pay and career history), an employee satisfaction survey, a manager
rating, and swipe-in and swipe-out times for every working day of 2015.
Section~\ref{sec:inspection} describes the files in detail.

\subsection{Methodology}

The analysis follows the steps recommended for the project:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data inspection} -- consistency, keys, encoding, data types,
  missing values and duplicates of the five files
  (Section~\ref{sec:inspection}).
  \item \textbf{Loading, integration and cleaning} -- merging on
  \texttt{EmployeeID}, imputation, type conversion and conversion of the
  attendance logs into working-hour and absence features
  (Section~\ref{sec:cleaning}).
  \item \textbf{Exploratory data analysis and feature selection} --
  distributions, outliers, correlations, hypothesis tests, and preparation of
  13 features (Section~\ref{sec:eda-fs}).
  \item \textbf{Clustering} -- K-Means, Ward hierarchical clustering and
  DBSCAN (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation} -- Elbow method, Silhouette, Davies--Bouldin and
  Calinski--Harabasz indices, baselines, stability, sensitivity and the
  effect of replicated records (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
  \item \textbf{Profiling and external validation} with \texttt{Attrition}
  (Section~\ref{sec:profiling}), followed by conclusions and recommendations
  (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data Cleaning}
\label{sec:cleaning}

The inspection in the previous section showed missing values in the
\texttt{general}, \texttt{employee}, \texttt{in\_time} and
\texttt{out\_time} files, no duplicate records, and attendance dates stored
as strings. This section describes how each problem was handled to obtain the
final table \texttt{df\_clean}. The pipeline is implemented in the
accompanying notebook.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Merging the Tables}

The \texttt{general}, \texttt{employee} and \texttt{manager} files were joined
on \texttt{EmployeeID} with \texttt{validate=}\allowbreak\texttt{"one\_to\_one"}, which raises an
error if any key is repeated. The merged table keeps all 4,410 employees and
contains 28 columns. The two attendance files are processed separately
(Section~\ref{sec:attendance}) and joined at the end.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Removing Duplicates}

Duplicates were checked again after merging, because joins can silently
multiply rows. Two criteria were used: fully identical rows and repeated
\texttt{EmployeeID}. Both checks returned 0, so no record was removed and the
table keeps 4,410 employees.

A third check compared the rows of \texttt{general} \emph{without} the
identifier. Here 2,912 rows (66\%) are identical to another row in every
column except \texttt{EmployeeID}: the file contains only 1,498 distinct
profiles, most of them three times. The copies have different survey answers
and attendance logs, so they are not plain duplicates and were kept. Their
effect on the models is analysed in Section~\ref{sec:replicated}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

The General and Employee datasets contain 28 (0.63\%) and 83 (1.88\%) missing

\paragraph{Count variables: median.}
\texttt{NumCompaniesWorked} and \texttt{TotalWorkingYears} are non-negative
counts. Figure~\ref{fig:figure1} shows that both are right-skewed (skewness

($11.28$ vs.\ $10$ years; $2.69$ vs.\ $2$ companies), so the mean no longer
describes a typical employee, and for an integer count it also produces
non-integer values. The median is not affected by the tail, so it was used.

\subsection{Processing Attendance Data}
\label{sec:attendance}

Each of \texttt{in\_time} and \texttt{out\_time} has 261 date columns and
109,080 missing cells. These are \emph{not} random gaps. A cell is empty when
the employee did not swipe on that day, that is, on a public holiday, on leave
or when absent. Filling them with a mean or median would invent working hours
that never happened. The cells were therefore handled in five steps:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Convert to datetime.} All 261 date columns were parsed
  with \texttt{pd.to\_datetime}.

  \item \textbf{Remove non-working days.} Days on which no employee has a
  swipe-in and swipe-out are public holidays or closed days and carry no
  information. 12 such columns (the float columns in
  Table~\ref{tab:data-types}) were dropped. They account for
  $12 \times 4{,}410 = 52{,}920$ of the missing cells in each file, and the
  remaining $109{,}080 - 52{,}920 = 56{,}160$ cells are individual absences.
  The number of working days is therefore $N_{\text{work}} = 249$.

  \item \textbf{Validate records.} A day is valid only if both swipe-in
  and swipe-out exist and the duration lies in $(0, 24]$ hours. The check
  found 0 days with only one of the two swipes and 0 days with an invalid
  duration, so no record had to be discarded.

  \item \textbf{Aggregate per employee.} The remaining missing days are
  skipped (not filled) when computing the features below.

  \item \textbf{Join.} The three features are merged into the main table
  on \texttt{EmployeeID} (one-to-one).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{align}
  h_{i,d} &= t^{\text{out}}_{i,d} - t^{\text{in}}_{i,d}
  \quad\text{(hours, valid days only)},\\
  \text{AvgWorkHours}_i &= \frac{1}{|D_i|}\sum_{d\in D_i} h_{i,d},\\
  \text{AbsentDays}_i &= N_{\text{work}} - |D_i|,\qquad
  \text{AbsentRate}_i = \frac{\text{AbsentDays}_i}{N_{\text{work}}},
\end{align}

where $D_i$ is the set of valid days of employee $i$. Every employee has at
least one valid day, so no median fallback was needed for these features.

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis and Feature Selection}
\label{sec:eda-fs}

This section examines the cleaned table \texttt{df\_clean}, selects the
variables used for clustering, and prepares them (encoding, scaling,

\subsection{Feature Preprocessing for Clustering}
\label{sec:prep}

\subsubsection{Encoding}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ordinal encoding.} \texttt{BusinessTravel} has a natural order
  and was mapped to Non-Travel $=0$, Travel\_Rarely $=1$,
  Travel\_Frequently $=2$.
  \item \textbf{One-hot encoding.} The five nominal variables (Department,
  JobRole, EducationField, MaritalStatus, Gender) have no order and were
  one-hot encoded into 23 binary columns. They are \emph{not} part of the

\section{Clustering Models}
\label{sec:models}

Three algorithms were applied to the matrix \texttt{X} of 4,410 employees
$\times$ 13 scaled features (Section~\ref{sec:prep}): K-Means,
Hierarchical clustering with Ward linkage, and DBSCAN. All results use the same
input and \texttt{random\_state=42}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}

Because no label exists, the clusterings are judged with internal indices.
For employee $i$, let $a(i)$ be the mean distance to the other members of its
cluster and $b(i)$ the mean distance to the nearest other cluster.

\begin{align}
  s(i) &= \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i), b(i)\}}, \qquad
  \text{Silhouette} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} s(i) \in [-1, 1],\\
  \text{WCSS} &= \sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{x \in C_j}\lVert x - \mu_j \rVert^2
  \quad\text{(inertia)}.
\end{align}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Silhouette} (higher is better): cohesion against separation.
  \item \textbf{Davies--Bouldin} (lower is better): average similarity between
  each cluster and its most similar cluster.
  \item \textbf{Calinski--Harabasz} (higher is better): ratio of between-cluster
  to within-cluster dispersion. It tends to fall as $k$ grows, so it is read
  together with the other indices.
  \item \textbf{Elbow (WCSS)}: the $k$ after which the decrease of inertia
  slows down.
  \item \textbf{Stability}: mean Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between K-Means
  models fitted on 10 random 80\% subsamples, each labelling all employees. A
  value near 1 means the same partition is found again.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reading the indices.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Elbow.} The inertia falls smoothly (the drop shrinks from 5.3\%
  to 1.7\% per extra cluster) and there is no sharp bend. The elbow method alone
  therefore does not give a clear $k$, which is typical of data without
  well-separated groups.
  \item \textbf{Silhouette.} The global maximum is at $k=2$ (0.103), followed
  by a plateau of 0.075--0.082 for $k=3,\dots,8$ with a local peak at $k=5$
  (0.082).
  \item \textbf{$k=2$ is too coarse.} The two clusters differ almost only in
  tenure (mean z-scores of \texttt{YearsSinceLastPromotion}, \texttt{YearsAtCompany}
  and \texttt{TotalWorkingYears} are $-0.53/{+}1.09$, $-0.48/{+}0.98$ and
  $-0.36/{+}0.74$ in the two clusters, while all other features stay within
  $\pm 0.1$). This corresponds to the first principal component
  found in the PCA and cannot represent the workload, commute and satisfaction
  profiles targeted by the project.
  \item \textbf{Choice.} Restricting the search to $k \ge 3$,
  $\mathbf{k = 5}$ has the highest silhouette (0.0819 against 0.0814 for
  $k=3$), the lowest Davies--Bouldin value among $k=3,4,5$ (2.666), and a
  high stability (ARI 0.82). $k=4$ and $k=6$ are clearly less stable (0.62 and
  0.70). The margin between $k=3$ and $k=5$ is small; $k=5$ was kept because it
  gives finer segments, and $k=3$ remains a valid coarser alternative
  (stability 0.85).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary and Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item K-Means with $k=5$ is the selected model (silhouette 0.082,
  Davies--Bouldin 2.666, stability ARI 0.82); $k=3$ is a close, more stable
  alternative.
  \item All indices are low in absolute terms (silhouette below 0.11 for every
  model and $k$). The data has weak group structure: the features are almost
  uncorrelated and the points form one continuous cloud. The clusters should be
  read as \emph{soft segments} along the main dimensions (tenure, workload,
  commute), not as natural, isolated groups.
  \item The indices alone cannot prove that $k=5$ is the true number of groups;
  the segments must be judged by their meaning and by external validation,
  which is done in Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation} and~\ref{sec:profiling} by
evaluating, profiling and comparing their
  attrition rates.
  \item DBSCAN highlights 105 high-level, long-hour employees with poor work-life
  balance and a 36.2\% attrition rate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Model Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The previous section selected K-Means with $k=5$ using internal indices. This
section checks whether that choice holds up from four further angles:
(i) comparison with baselines, (ii) stability, (iii) sensitivity to the
feature groups, and (iv) external validation against \texttt{Attrition}
(Section~\ref{sec:profiling}).

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Comparison with Baselines}

Two baselines were built. A model that cannot beat them has found nothing
useful:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random labels}: every employee is assigned to one of five
  groups at random (mean over 20 draws).
  \item \textbf{Unscaled K-Means}: K-Means ($k=5$) fitted on the raw features,
  without log transform or scaling.
\end{itemize}
All partitions are scored in the same scaled 13-feature space, so the values
are directly comparable.

  \item K-Means and Ward both clearly beat the \textbf{random baseline}

  essential, and the high silhouette of the raw data in
  Section~\ref{sec:prep} is an artefact of measuring it in an
  income-dominated space.

group hardly influences the segmentation; a low ARI means the group drives it.

\subsection{Effect of Replicated Records}
\label{sec:replicated}

As noted in Section~\ref{sec:cleaning}, the 4,410 employees come from only
1,498 distinct \texttt{general} profiles: 4,326 employees belong to a profile
that appears three times, 56 to a profile that appears twice and 28 are
unique. Copies always share the same \texttt{Attrition} value, but have
different survey answers and attendance logs. The Kaggle file was evidently
built by replicating a smaller HR table.

In the 13-feature clustering space the copies sit close together: for 92.2\%
of employees the nearest neighbour is a copy of themselves, and the median
distance between copies (1.09) is about 4.5 times smaller than between two
random employees (4.93). Table~\ref{tab:replicated} shows what happens when
only one copy per profile is kept.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{DBSCAN.} Every point automatically has two very close

  (ARI 0.51 with the final model), consistent with soft cluster borders.

  independent profiles, so the p-values of the hypothesis tests and of the
  chi-square test with attrition are smaller than they should be. Because the
  effects are large, the conclusions do not change.
  \item The records were kept, because each copy carries its own survey and
  attendance information.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Summary.} Five relational files describing 4{,}410 employees were
merged and cleaned, and the attendance logs were converted into working-hour
and absence features. Thirteen features covering seniority and pay, workload,
satisfaction and commute were log-transformed where skewed, standardised and
clustered with K-Means, Ward hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN.

\paragraph{Best model.} \textbf{K-Means with $k=5$} is the best model. It is
better than Ward on all internal indices, clearly beats the random and
unscaled baselines, is reasonably stable under bootstrap resampling (ARI 0.80)
and separates attrition better than Ward. DBSCAN finds no segmentation in this
continuous data but is useful as an anomaly detector: 2.4\% of employees,
mostly high-level staff with long working hours and poor work-life balance,
with 36\% attrition.

\textbf{How to use the segmentation in practice.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Assign every current and new employee to a segment with the fitted
  scaler and K-Means model.
  \item Concentrate the retention budget on the two high-risk segments. They
  hold 53\% of all leavers but only 29\% of the workforce, so the same effort
  reaches almost twice as many potential leavers as an untargeted programme.
  \item Review the 105 DBSCAN anomalies one by one: they combine long hours,
  poor work-life balance and a 36\% attrition rate.
  \item Repeat the clustering every year to see whether employees move out
  of the high-risk segments after the actions above.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The cluster structure is weak (silhouette 0.08): the segments are soft
  regions of one continuous cloud, and employees near a border could belong to
  a neighbouring segment.
  \item The satisfaction scores add no structure (removing them barely changes
  the partition), so the segmentation cannot identify satisfaction-based
  groups.
  \item The dataset is semi-synthetic: the 4,410 employees come from only
  1,498 distinct profiles (Section~\ref{sec:replicated}), which inflates the
  density used by DBSCAN and makes p-values optimistic; several attributes
  behave like independent random values; attendance covers only 2015; and
  K-Means assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar size.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Future work.} Gaussian Mixture Models (soft membership) or
K-Prototypes (numeric and categorical features together) could be tested; the
segments should be validated on real HR data; and they can be combined with a
supervised attrition model to target retention actions at individual
employees.
