\begin{abstract}
This report applies unsupervised learning to the \emph{HR Analytics Case Study}
dataset, which describes about 4{,}410 employees of a company through
demographic, job, survey and attendance (swipe-in/swipe-out) records.

The goal is to discover naturally occurring employee segments without using
any pre-defined label. We integrate the relational files, clean and engineer
features (including average daily working hours derived from attendance logs),
and compare three clustering algorithms --- K-Means, Hierarchical (Ward)
clustering and DBSCAN --- using the Silhouette score, the Elbow method,
Davies--Bouldin and Calinski--Harabasz indices.

The resulting segments are profiled and cross-checked against the
\texttt{Attrition} column (used only for external validation) to derive
practical HR recommendations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data Cleaning}

The inspection in the previous section showed missing values in the
\texttt{general}, \texttt{employee}, \texttt{in\_time} and
\texttt{out\_time} files, no duplicate records, and attendance dates stored
as strings. This section describes how each problem was handled to obtain the
final table \texttt{df\_clean}. The pipeline is implemented in the
accompanying notebook.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Merging the Tables}

The \texttt{general}, \texttt{employee} and \texttt{manager} files were joined
on \texttt{EmployeeID} with \texttt{validate=}\allowbreak\texttt{"one\_to\_one"}, which raises an
error if any key is repeated. The merged table keeps all 4,410 employees and
contains 28 columns. The two attendance files are processed separately
(Section~\ref{sec:attendance}) and joined at the end.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Removing Duplicates}

Duplicates were checked again after merging, because joins can silently
multiply rows. Two criteria were used: fully identical rows and repeated
\texttt{EmployeeID}. Both checks returned 0, so no record was removed and the
table keeps 4,410 employees.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Processing Attendance Data}
\label{sec:attendance}

Each of \texttt{in\_time} and \texttt{out\_time} has 261 date columns and
109,080 missing cells. These are \emph{not} random gaps. A cell is empty when
the employee did not swipe on that day, that is, on a public holiday, on leave
or when absent. Filling them with a mean or median would invent working hours
that never happened. The cells were therefore handled in five steps:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Convert to datetime.} All 261 date columns were parsed
  with \texttt{pd.to\_datetime}.

  \item \textbf{Remove non-working days.} Days on which no employee has a
  swipe-in and swipe-out are public holidays or closed days and carry no
  information. 12 such columns (the float columns in
  Table~\ref{tab:data-types}) were dropped. They account for
  $12 \times 4{,}410 = 52{,}920$ of the missing cells in each file, and the
  remaining $109{,}080 - 52{,}920 = 56{,}160$ cells are individual absences.
  The number of working days is therefore $N_{\text{work}} = 249$.

  \item \textbf{Validate records.} A day is valid only if both swipe-in
  and swipe-out exist and the duration lies in $(0, 24]$ hours. The check
  found 0 days with only one of the two swipes and 0 days with an invalid
  duration, so no record had to be discarded.

  \item \textbf{Aggregate per employee.} The remaining missing days are
  skipped (not filled) when computing the features below.

  \item \textbf{Join.} The three features are merged into the main table
  on \texttt{EmployeeID} (one-to-one).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{align}
  h_{i,d} &= t^{\text{out}}_{i,d} - t^{\text{in}}_{i,d}
  \quad\text{(hours, valid days only)},\\
  \text{AvgWorkHours}_i &= \frac{1}{|D_i|}\sum_{d\in D_i} h_{i,d},\\
  \text{AbsentDays}_i &= N_{\text{work}} - |D_i|,\qquad
  \text{AbsentRate}_i = \frac{\text{AbsentDays}_i}{N_{\text{work}}},
\end{align}

where $D_i$ is the set of valid days of employee $i$. Every employee has at
least one valid day, so no median fallback was needed for these features.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Core of the attendance cleaning step}]
in_dt  = in_df.apply(pd.to_datetime, errors="coerce")
out_dt = out_df.apply(pd.to_datetime, errors="coerce")

# drop public holidays: days with no swipe for every employee
holiday = in_dt.columns[in_dt.isna().all() & out_dt.isna().all()]
in_dt, out_dt = in_dt.drop(columns=holiday), out_dt.drop(columns=holiday)

# keep only days with both in and out
half = in_dt.isna() ^ out_dt.isna()
in_dt, out_dt = in_dt.mask(half), out_dt.mask(half)

hours = (out_dt - in_dt).apply(lambda s: s.dt.total_seconds() / 3600)
hours = hours.mask((hours <= 0) | (hours > 24))

att = pd.DataFrame({
    "AvgWorkHours": hours.mean(axis=1),          # NaN days skipped
    "AbsentDays":   hours.isna().sum(axis=1),
})
att["AbsentRate"] = att["AbsentDays"] / hours.shape[1]
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Encoding}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ordinal encoding.} \texttt{BusinessTravel} has a natural order
  and was mapped to Non-Travel $=0$, Travel\_Rarely $=1$,
  Travel\_Frequently $=2$.
  \item \textbf{One-hot encoding.} The five nominal variables (Department,
  JobRole, EducationField, MaritalStatus, Gender) have no order and were

\section{Clustering Models}

Three algorithms were applied to the matrix \texttt{X} of 4,410 employees
$\times$ 13 scaled features (Section~\ref{sec:prep}): K-Means,
Hierarchical clustering with Ward linkage, and DBSCAN. All results use the same
input and \texttt{random\_state=42}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}

Because no label exists, the clusterings are judged with internal indices.
For employee $i$, let $a(i)$ be the mean distance to the other members of its
cluster and $b(i)$ the mean distance to the nearest other cluster.

\begin{align}
  s(i) &= \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i), b(i)\}}, \qquad
  \text{Silhouette} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} s(i) \in [-1, 1],\\
  \text{WCSS} &= \sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{x \in C_j}\lVert x - \mu_j \rVert^2
  \quad\text{(inertia)}.
\end{align}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Silhouette} (higher is better): cohesion against separation.
  \item \textbf{Davies--Bouldin} (lower is better): average similarity between
  each cluster and its most similar cluster.
  \item \textbf{Calinski--Harabasz} (higher is better): ratio of between-cluster
  to within-cluster dispersion. It tends to fall as $k$ grows, so it is read
  together with the other indices.
  \item \textbf{Elbow (WCSS)}: the $k$ after which the decrease of inertia
  slows down.
  \item \textbf{Stability}: mean Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between K-Means
  models fitted on 10 random 80\% subsamples, each labelling all employees. A
  value near 1 means the same partition is found again.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reading the indices.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Elbow.} The inertia falls smoothly (the drop shrinks from 5.3\%

  therefore does not give a clear $k$, which is typical of data without

  by a plateau of 0.075--0.082 for $k=3,\dots,8$ with a local peak at $k=5$

\subsection{Summary and Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item K-Means with $k=5$ is the selected model (silhouette 0.082,
  Davies--Bouldin 2.666, stability ARI 0.82); $k=3$ is a close, more stable
  alternative.
  \item All indices are low in absolute terms (silhouette below 0.11 for every
  model and $k$). The data has weak group structure: the features are almost
  uncorrelated and the points form one continuous cloud. The clusters should be
  read as \emph{soft segments} along the main dimensions (tenure, workload,
  commute), not as natural, isolated groups.
  \item The indices alone cannot prove that $k=5$ is the true number of groups;
  the segments must be judged by their meaning and by external validation,
  which is done in the next section by profiling them and comparing their
  attrition rates.
  \item DBSCAN highlights 105 high-level, long-hour employees with poor work-life
  balance and a 36.2\% attrition rate.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item K-Means and Ward both clearly beat the \textbf{random baseline}
  (silhouette $\approx 0$, Calinski--Harabasz $\approx 1$). The partitions
  capture real, if weak, structure.
  \item The \textbf{unscaled} K-Means only cuts \texttt{MonthlyIncome} into
  bands: its between-cluster spread is 1.31 standard deviations for income and
  at most 0.10 for any other feature. In the scaled space this partition is
  barely better than random (silhouette 0.010). Scaling is therefore
  essential, and the high silhouette of the raw data in
  Section~\ref{sec:prep} is an artefact of measuring it in an
  income-dominated space.
  \item \textbf{K-Means is better than Ward on all three indices}. DBSCAN
  returns one cluster and cannot be scored.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Summary.} Five relational files describing 4{,}410 employees were
merged and cleaned, and the attendance logs were converted into working-hour
and absence features. Thirteen features covering seniority and pay, workload,
satisfaction and commute were log-transformed where skewed, standardised and
clustered with K-Means, Ward hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN.

\paragraph{Best model.} \textbf{K-Means with $k=5$} is the best model. It is
better than Ward on all internal indices, clearly beats the random and
unscaled baselines, is reasonably stable under bootstrap resampling (ARI 0.80)
and separates attrition better than Ward. DBSCAN finds no segmentation in this
continuous data but is useful as an anomaly detector: 2.4\% of employees,
mostly high-level staff with long working hours and poor work-life balance,
with 36\% attrition.

\paragraph{Practical implications.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Newcomers} are the main retention risk: strengthen onboarding,
  mentoring and a clear first-promotion path during the first two years.
  \item \emph{Overtime workers} leave 1.45 times more often than average:
  monitor working hours, rebalance workload and limit sustained overtime,
  since satisfaction surveys alone do not reveal this group.
  \item \emph{Stagnant veterans} have waited about seven years for promotion;
  recognition, expert career tracks or job rotation can keep them engaged.
  \item \emph{Long commuters} are not a high-risk group today, but flexible or
  remote work could protect them.
  \item The \emph{steady core} is stable and can provide mentors for
  newcomers.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The cluster structure is weak (silhouette 0.08): the segments are soft
  regions of one continuous cloud, and employees near a border could belong to
  a neighbouring segment.
  \item The satisfaction scores add no structure (removing them barely changes
  the partition), so the segmentation cannot identify satisfaction-based
  groups.
  \item The dataset is semi-synthetic (many replicated records; several
  attributes behave like independent random values), attendance covers only
  2015, and K-Means assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar size.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Future work.} Gaussian Mixture Models (soft membership) or
K-Prototypes (numeric and categorical features together) could be tested; the
segments should be validated on real HR data; and they can be combined with a
supervised attrition model to target retention actions at individual
employees.
